Piles and rectal bleeding: when to get checked

Seeing blood after a bowel movement is unsettling. Piles, also called haemorrhoids or bawaseer, are one possible explanation for bright red bleeding. Bleeding has other causes too. Arrange an assessment if it is new, keeps returning or you are unsure of the cause. Heavy bleeding, large clots or bleeding that will not stop needs emergency care.

Describe what actually happened

A clinician may ask whether the blood was on the tissue, on the outside of the stool or mixed through it. Mention pain, itching, a lump or a change in your bowel habits. If you take medicines that affect bleeding, take the names and doses with you. Do not stop a prescribed medicine on your own.

Asking for privacy

If it feels uncomfortable to explain the problem at reception, ask to discuss the details with the clinician. You can write a brief note in the language you are comfortable using. Include when it began and how often it has happened. That gives the consultation a clear starting point.

What can help if piles are confirmed?

Keeping stools soft and avoiding straining can reduce irritation. The NHS advises adequate fluid, fibre and avoiding long periods sitting on the toilet. In a Pakistani household, examples of fibre-containing foods include daal, vegetables and wholegrain roti. If you have been given dietary or fluid restrictions, ask how to adapt this advice.

When symptoms continue

If symptoms persist, ask whether the diagnosis needs checking again and what treatments might suit you. Explain what you have already tried, including pharmacy preparations, and whether the bleeding changed. Before leaving, ask what improvement to expect, when to return and what level of bleeding should prompt an urgent visit.
